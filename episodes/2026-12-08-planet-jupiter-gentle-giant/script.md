# A Wandering Light in the Ancient Sky
@image commons:File:Starry Night Over the Rhone.jpg
Long before telescopes, long before spacecraft, people already knew Jupiter. They did not know it was a planet made of gas. They knew it only as a light. A steady, bright, golden-white light, moving very slowly among the stars.

On a clear night, Jupiter is one of the brightest things in the sky. Only the Moon and Venus, and sometimes Mars, can shine more brightly. It does not twinkle very much, the way the stars do. It glows calmly, like a lamp left on in a distant window.

@image commons:File:19th century Hipparchus engraving.jpg
The ancient Greeks noticed that a few lights in the sky did not stay in place. Most stars kept their patterns, night after night, year after year. But five bright lights wandered slowly through those patterns. The Greeks called them planetes, which means wanderers. That is where our word planet comes from.

Jupiter is a slow wanderer. It takes almost twelve years to travel once all the way around the sky, through all the constellations of the zodiac. So, in many years, it stays in roughly one constellation, as if it were visiting a different house each year.

@image commons:File:British Museum, Babylon Astronomical Diary, 331-330 BC.jpg
In ancient Babylon, in the land between the Tigris and Euphrates rivers, astronomers watched the planets with great care. They pressed their notes into soft clay with a reed, in the wedge-shaped writing we call cuneiform. They wrote down where each planet rose, where it stopped, and where it seemed to turn back.

For the Babylonians, Jupiter was connected with Marduk, the protector god of their city. Perhaps that is one reason they studied it so closely.

@image commons:File:Processional Way, Babylon - Google Art Project.jpg
In 2016, a historian of science named Mathieu Ossendrijver described a few small clay tablets now kept at the British Museum in London. They had been written by Babylonian astronomers more than two thousand years ago. On them, the astronomers calculated how far Jupiter moved across the sky over sixty days.

They did it in a clever way. They drew, in numbers, a shape like a trapezoid, a box with one slanted side. The area of that shape gave them the distance that Jupiter travelled.

@image commons:File:Pope Sylvester II (Gerbert d'Aurillac) - De geometria.jpg
This kind of thinking, linking speed, time and distance through a shape, appears again in Europe only more than a thousand years later, in the work of scholars in Oxford and Paris. Historians were surprised. Somewhere in Babylon, someone had looked up at Jupiter, and then looked down at a piece of clay, and found a beautiful idea.

There is something peaceful in that picture. A person sitting quietly under the stars, watching one slow light, and writing down its steps, night after night.

@image commons:File:Standbeeld van Jupiter, RP-P-1896-A-19368-514.jpg
Later, the Romans gave this bright planet the name we still use today. They named it after Jupiter, the king of their gods, the god of the sky and of thunder. For the Greeks, the same god was Zeus.

It was a fitting name, even though no one could know it then. Of all the wandering lights, this one would turn out to be the largest world of all. The king of the planets.

@image commons:File:Jan Miel - Market Scene at the Foot of the Capitoline Hill in Rome.jpg
In Rome, the great temple of Jupiter stood on the Capitoline Hill, above the Forum. People climbed the steps to make offerings there. And on clear nights, some of them must have looked up and seen the planet with the same name, shining quietly over their city.

We still remember the god in our calendar, too. In several languages, one day of the week is named after Jupiter. In Italian it is giovedì. In Spanish it is jueves. In French it is jeudi. Even the English word Thursday belongs to this family. It is named after Thor, the Norse god of thunder, whom people long ago matched with Jupiter.

@image commons:File:Cellarius ptolemaic system.jpg
For a very long time, people believed that every wandering light moved around the Earth. The Earth was at the center, still and solid, and the heavens turned around it. Jupiter, in this picture, rode on a great invisible sphere, far above the Moon, the Sun and Mars.

That picture was beautiful, and it lasted for many centuries. But one winter night, in the year sixteen ten, a man in Italy pointed a new instrument at Jupiter. And the sky began to change.

# Galileo and the Four Little Stars
@image commons:File:Galileo Galilei (1564-1642) RMG BHC2700.tiff
The man was Galileo Galilei, a professor of mathematics in Padua, in the north of Italy. In the autumn of sixteen oh nine, he had begun to build telescopes of his own. He ground the glass lenses himself, slowly, by hand. His best instruments made distant things look around twenty times closer.

They were simple tubes, covered in leather or paper, a little longer than a man's arm. Today, two of them still survive, in a museum in Florence.

@image commons:File:Galilei telescopes, Museo Galileo, Florence, Inv. 242, 2428, 224088.jpg
With these tubes, Galileo looked at the Moon and saw mountains and valleys. He looked at the pale band of the Milky Way and saw that it was made of countless faint stars. And on the seventh of January, sixteen ten, he looked at Jupiter.

Next to the bright planet, he saw three small points of light, sitting in a straight line. He thought they were ordinary stars, far behind Jupiter.

@image commons:File:Medicean Stars.png
But the next night, the little stars had moved. Now they were in different places. Galileo kept watching, night after night, and he drew what he saw: a small circle for Jupiter, and little stars beside it, sometimes to the left, sometimes to the right. Sometimes one was missing. A few nights later, he saw a fourth.

Slowly, he understood. These were not stars at all. They were moons, travelling around Jupiter, the way our Moon travels around the Earth.

@image commons:File:Sidereus, nuncius magna longeqve admirabilia spectacula... Wellcome L0072630.jpg
Galileo wrote quickly. Only a few weeks later, in Venice, he published a short book called Sidereus Nuncius, which means the Starry Messenger. He dedicated it to Cosimo the Second de' Medici, the young Grand Duke of Tuscany. And he named the four new moons the Medicean Stars, after the Medici family.

The gift worked. Soon afterwards, Galileo became the court mathematician and philosopher in Florence.

@image commons:File:View of Florence and Arno river (4).jpg
But the moons meant much more than a new job. For centuries, many people had believed that everything in the heavens turned around the Earth. Here, suddenly, were four small worlds that clearly turned around something else.

This did not prove, all by itself, that the Earth moves around the Sun. But it showed that the old picture was not complete. The sky was larger, and stranger, and more interesting than anyone had thought.

@image commons:File:Simon Marius (Mayr). Woodcut, 1614. Wellcome V0003861.jpg
Galileo was not the only person looking. In Germany, an astronomer named Simon Marius also watched these moons. In sixteen fourteen, he wrote that he had seen them in the very first days of sixteen ten, almost at the same time as Galileo. Historians still discuss his claim, because he made it four years later.

But Marius gave us something lasting. After thinking about a suggestion passed on by the astronomer Johannes Kepler, he proposed names for the four moons.

@image cma:154645
He took the names from old Greek stories about the god Zeus, the Greek Jupiter. The four moons became Io, Europa, Ganymede and Callisto. For about three hundred years, astronomers mostly ignored these names and simply used numbers. Jupiter One, Jupiter Two, and so on.

Only in the twentieth century, when spacecraft showed that each moon was a very different world, did the old names truly come back to life.

@image commons:File:Le Monde Illustré - Observatoire de Paris - 5 mars 1870 - 1.jpg
Jupiter's moons also helped answer a question that seems to have nothing to do with Jupiter at all. How fast does light travel?

In the sixteen seventies, at the new Royal Observatory in Paris, astronomers watched the moon Io very carefully. Io passes behind Jupiter on every orbit, about every forty-two hours, and disappears into its shadow. These small eclipses were so regular that sailors hoped to use them like a clock in the sky.

@image commons:File:Rømer, Ole (ur Berømte danske maend).jpg
A young Danish astronomer, Ole Rømer, noticed something curious. The eclipses were not perfectly on time. When the Earth was moving away from Jupiter, they came a little late. When the Earth was moving toward Jupiter, they came a little early.

In sixteen seventy-six, Rømer predicted that an eclipse in November would be about ten minutes late. And it was.

@image commons:File:Jupiter, Io and Europa - Voyager 1 (29275215195).png
His explanation was simple and beautiful. Light does not arrive in an instant. It needs time to travel. When the Earth is farther from Jupiter, the light from Io simply has a longer road to walk.

Rømer's numbers were not perfect, and some astronomers did not accept his idea at first. But he was right. Light has a speed. And a small moon, going quietly behind a giant planet, was the first to tell us so.

# How Big Is Jupiter, Really?
@image commons:File:Jupiter, Earth size comparison.jpg
Let us try to imagine the size of Jupiter. It is not easy, because it is larger than anything we know in daily life.

Imagine that the Earth is a small grape, resting in the palm of your hand. On the same scale, Jupiter would be about the size of a basketball. Across its middle, Jupiter is about eleven times wider than the Earth. If you could walk around its equator, the journey would be about eleven times longer than a walk around our own world.

@image commons:File:Solar system scale edit.jpg
And if Jupiter were a hollow ball, you could pour around a thousand Earths inside it, and still have room to spare.

Jupiter is also heavy. It holds more than twice as much mass as all the other planets put together. Take Saturn, Uranus, Neptune, the Earth, Venus, Mars and Mercury. Put them all together on one side of a giant scale. Jupiter alone, on the other side, would be heavier than all of them, twice over.

@image commons:File:Full disk of the Sun (eso1703e-comparisona).jpg
And yet, next to the Sun, even Jupiter is small. The Sun is about ten times wider than Jupiter, and about a thousand times heavier. Jupiter is a giant among planets, but it is still a child of the Sun.

People sometimes call Jupiter a failed star. It is a gentle idea, but it is not quite right. To shine like a star, a world would need to be many, many times heavier than Jupiter. Jupiter is not a star that failed. It is simply a very large planet, and a very good one.

@image commons:File:Hubble View of Jupiter and Europa.jpg
Jupiter turns very quickly. One day on Jupiter, from one sunrise to the next, lasts less than ten hours. That is the shortest day of any planet in our Solar System. If you could sleep on Jupiter, you would barely close your eyes before the Sun came back.

Because it spins so fast, Jupiter is not perfectly round. Its middle bulges outward a little, and its top and bottom are a little flat, like a ball of soft dough turning on a potter's wheel.

@image commons:File:Solar System Diagram, updated (26328778460).jpg
In 2026, scientists working with the Juno spacecraft measured this shape more carefully than ever before. They found that Jupiter is a tiny bit narrower and flatter than people had believed for nearly fifty years. Only by a few kilometers, on a world more than a hundred and forty thousand kilometers wide. But even giants, it seems, can still surprise us.

While its days are short, its years are long. Jupiter takes almost twelve Earth years to travel once around the Sun.

@image commons:File:Jupiter - January 5 2001 (49123569506).png
A child born on Earth on the same day that Jupiter began a new orbit would be nearly twelve years old when Jupiter came back to the same place. In a whole human life, Jupiter goes around the Sun only six or seven times.

Jupiter lives far from the Sun. On average, it is about seven hundred and seventy-eight million kilometers away. That is about five times farther than the Earth. Sunlight, which reaches us in about eight minutes, needs around forty-three minutes to reach Jupiter.

@image commons:File:PIA21972 Jupiter Blues.jpg
So the light out there is soft and weak. The Sun, seen from Jupiter, would look like a small, very bright star. It would give only about four percent of the warmth and light that we feel here. A gentle, distant glow, always far away.

Jupiter's gravity is strong. If you could stand somewhere at the top of its clouds, you would feel about two and a half times heavier than you do on Earth.

@image commons:File:Jupiter - PJ27-40-42-45 (50016009883).png
But you could not really stand on Jupiter. There is no ground, no rock beneath your feet. Jupiter is a world of gas and liquid, mostly hydrogen and helium, the two lightest elements in the universe. As you went deeper, the air would only grow thicker and warmer and darker, without ever reaching a floor.

It is a planet with no surface at all. Only clouds above clouds, and an ocean of strange, hot liquid far below.

@image commons:File:Jupiter Cylindrical Map - Dec 2000 PIA07782.jpg
Jupiter is also tilted only a tiny amount, about three degrees. The Earth is tilted much more, and that is why we have summer and winter. On Jupiter, the Sun stays almost exactly over the equator all year long.

So Jupiter has no real seasons. Its long year passes evenly, without spring blossoms or winter snow. Just the same steady turning, again and again, every ten hours, for twelve years at a time.

# Ribbons of Cloud: The Belts and Zones of Jupiter
@image commons:File:Jupiter, image taken by NASA's Hubble Space Telescope, June 2019.png
Even through a small telescope, Jupiter does not look like a plain white ball. It looks striped. Across its face run soft bands of color, cream and tan, brown and pale orange, like the layers in a smooth stone from a river.

Astronomers give these stripes two names. The lighter bands are called zones. The darker bands are called belts. They wrap all the way around the planet, parallel to the equator, like ribbons tied around a gift.

@image commons:File:PIA09339 Jupiter's High-Altitude Clouds.jpg
In the light zones, the air is generally rising. As it rises, it cools, and pale clouds form high up, catching the sunlight. In the darker belts, the air is generally sinking. The high clouds thin out there, and we can see a little deeper, into warmer and browner layers below.

So when you look at Jupiter's stripes, you are really looking at a great, slow breathing of the atmosphere. Rising air and falling air, side by side, all the way around the world.

@image commons:File:Jupiter (NIRCam Image) (2023-147).png
Scientists believe that Jupiter has three main layers of cloud, stacked one above the other. At the top, where it is coldest, there are clouds made of tiny crystals of ammonia ice. Below them is a layer made of a compound of ammonia and sulfur. And deeper still, there are clouds made of water, like the clouds we know at home.

All three layers together are only around seventy kilometers thick. A thin skin of weather, on a world that is enormous.

@image commons:File:Jupiter - PJ17-2.jpeg
Nobody is completely sure why Jupiter's clouds have their colors. The pure ammonia ice should be white. The browns, oranges and reds probably come from small amounts of other chemicals, changed by sunlight, like paint mixed into cream. Scientists still discuss which chemicals they are.

Whatever their cause, the colors are always changing a little. A belt can fade to pale gold over a few months, and then darken again. Jupiter is never quite the same twice.

@image commons:File:PIA23809 - Deep Jet Streams in Jupiter's Atmosphere.jpg
Between the belts and zones, the winds are strong. They blow east and west, in long, steady streams, some of them faster than anything on Earth. Near the equator, some winds reach more than five hundred kilometers per hour.

But these winds are very orderly. Each stream keeps its own lane, year after year, like a calm, endless river flowing beside another river going the other way.

@image commons:File:Juno & Jupiter (30273927457).jpg
For a long time, scientists wondered how deep these winds went. Were they only a thin layer on the surface, like a breeze over the sea? Or did they reach far down into the planet?

The Juno spacecraft helped to answer this. By measuring Jupiter's gravity very carefully, it found that the jet streams reach down about three thousand kilometers. Deep, but still only a small part of the whole planet.

@image nasaid:PIA22474
In the deep water clouds, Jupiter has storms with lightning, flashing in the dark. And Juno's scientists have suggested something even stranger. High up, they think, there may be soft balls of slushy ice, made of water and ammonia, falling slowly like hail. They call them mushballs.

It is a funny little word for a very large place. Somewhere on Jupiter, at this very moment, it may be quietly raining mushballs.

@image commons:File:Parachute deployment.jpeg
Only once has a human-made object fallen gently into Jupiter's clouds and spoken to us from inside them. On the seventh of December, nineteen ninety-five, a small probe from the Galileo spacecraft entered the atmosphere.

It slowed down, opened a parachute, and drifted down through the cloud layers. For about fifty-eight minutes, it sent back measurements of temperature, pressure, wind and air. Then the heat and pressure grew too great, and it fell silent.

@image commons:File:Galileo Probe Entry.jpg
The probe brought surprises. It found less water than scientists expected, and drier, clearer air. Later, scientists understood that the probe had fallen into an unusual place, a hot, dry spot, like a gap in the clouds. It was a little like trying to learn about the Earth's weather by landing once in a desert.

Still, for those fifty-eight minutes, a small machine from Earth floated inside a giant. Nothing like it had ever happened before.

# The Great Red Spot, an Old Storm
@image commons:File:Great red spot juno 20170712.jpg
In the southern half of Jupiter, there is a large oval, the color of salmon, or of a pale brick in the evening sun. It is called the Great Red Spot. It is a storm. A single, enormous, slowly turning storm, about as wide as the whole planet Earth.

On Earth, a big storm might last a few days, or perhaps a few weeks. The Great Red Spot has lasted for at least a hundred and ninety years. It may be the longest-lived storm that anyone has ever watched.

@image commons:File:Jupiter from Voyager 1.jpg
The storm turns counterclockwise. It goes around once every few Earth days. The winds at its edge are fast, but the center is surprisingly calm, a quiet eye in the middle of all that movement.

Around it, the white and brown clouds of Jupiter flow past, like water in a stream flowing around a large stone. Sometimes small storms drift too close, and the great oval swallows them, or pulls them around its edge.

@image commons:File:Giovanni Domenico Cassini, Biblioteca Aprosiana.jpg
The story of the Great Red Spot begins, perhaps, in sixteen sixty-five. That year, an Italian astronomer named Giovanni Domenico Cassini saw a dark oval on Jupiter, at the same latitude as today's red spot. He followed it for years, and called it the Permanent Spot.

Other astronomers watched it too, until seventeen thirteen. And then, it seems, nobody saw it again.

@image commons:File:The elements of astronomy; a textbook (1919) (14802110823).jpg
For more than a hundred years, there are no clear records of a great spot on Jupiter. Then, in eighteen thirty-one, astronomers began to record an oval there once again. And from that year onward, we have been able to follow it almost without a break.

Was it the same spot that Cassini saw? In 2024, a team of scientists studied all the old drawings and notes. They concluded that it probably was not. Cassini's spot most likely faded away, and the storm we see today was born later, around the early nineteenth century.

@image commons:File:Trouvelot - Jupiter 1880.tif
In the late eighteen hundreds, the Great Red Spot became famous. Telescopes were better now, and many people looked at it. One French artist and astronomer, Étienne Léopold Trouvelot, made beautiful pastel drawings of Jupiter, showing the great oval glowing in its belt.

In those years, the spot was huge. In eighteen seventy-nine, it was about thirty-nine thousand kilometers long. Three Earths could have fit across it, side by side.

@image commons:File:NASA's Hubble Shows Jupiter's Great Red Spot is Smaller than Ever (14192178934).jpg
Since then, it has been shrinking. Slowly, quietly, it has become smaller and rounder. Today it is about fourteen thousand kilometers long, roughly as wide as the Earth.

No one knows for certain what will happen next. Perhaps it will keep shrinking. Perhaps it will settle at a smaller size and stay for centuries more. Scientists watch it with patience, the way you might watch an old tree in a garden.

@image nasaid:PIA24817
How deep is the Great Red Spot? For a long time, nobody knew. Then, in 2021, scientists used Juno's instruments, which can sense heat from deep below the clouds. They found that the storm reaches down about five hundred kilometers.

That is very deep for a storm. If the Great Red Spot sat on the Earth, it would rise far above the air we breathe, far above where airplanes fly, and up into the dark of space.

@image commons:File:Jupiter's Great Red Spot mosaic from Voyager 1.jpg
Why is it red? Again, the answer is not completely known. The storm lifts gases high into the cold air above the clouds. There, the faint sunlight may change them, little by little, into reddish chemicals, a little like the way an apple slowly turns brown in the air.

Some years the spot looks deep orange. Other years, it fades to a soft, pale pink. It is never exactly the same color twice.

@image commons:File:Jupiter third red spot.jpg
The Great Red Spot is not alone. Jupiter has many other ovals, white and brown and red. One of them, born in the year two thousand when three smaller white storms joined together, turned red a few years later. Astronomers sometimes call it Red Spot Junior.

So the old storm has a little companion now. Two red ovals, drifting through the southern clouds, slowly turning, slowly changing, on a world where the weather never stops.

# Deep Inside Jupiter: Oceans of Hydrogen and a Fuzzy Core
@image nasaid:PIA25062
Now let us sink, slowly and gently, beneath the clouds of Jupiter. Imagine that we are protected, warm and safe, inside a small, quiet ship that can go anywhere.

At first, there is only darkness and haze. The clouds are above us now. The air grows thicker, like moving through warm syrup. The pressure rises, and the temperature rises with it. Below the lowest clouds, there is no more sunlight at all.

@image commons:File:Jupiter - PJ27-58 - South Pole (50019444403).png
As we go deeper, something strange happens. The hydrogen gas around us is pressed so hard that it gradually becomes a liquid. There is no clear line where the sky ends and the ocean begins. The gas simply becomes thicker and thicker, until it behaves like a liquid. We are drifting into the largest ocean in the Solar System.

It is not an ocean of water. It is an ocean of hydrogen, deep and hot, and completely dark.

@image commons:File:Jupiter North Pole - Juno Perijove 11 - February 7 2018 (40126690132).jpg
Deeper still, perhaps halfway down to the center, the pressure becomes almost unimaginable. Here, scientists believe, hydrogen changes again. It becomes so tightly packed that it can carry electricity, like a metal. They call it metallic hydrogen.

You would not recognize it as metal. It is not a solid bar of iron. It is a hot, flowing liquid that can carry electric currents, and it fills a huge part of the inside of Jupiter.

@image commons:File:Juno orbit around Jupiter.jpg
And at the very center? For many years, scientists imagined a small, solid core of rock and ice, like a hard seed in the middle of a soft fruit.

But the Juno spacecraft found something different. By measuring Jupiter's gravity again and again, it showed that the center seems to be fuzzy. Heavier materials are not packed into one neat ball. They seem to be spread out, mixed with hydrogen and helium, across a region that may reach nearly half of the planet's width.

@image commons:File:Jupiter Showcases Auroras, Hazes (NIRCam Closeup).jpg
Scientists still discuss why the core is like this. Perhaps it was always fuzzy. Perhaps, long ago, something stirred it. It is one of the quiet mysteries still waiting inside the giant.

Jupiter is also warm inside. In fact, it sends out more heat into space than it receives from the faraway Sun. Some of this warmth is left over from its birth, more than four billion years ago. Jupiter has been slowly cooling ever since, and very slowly settling, like a fire that still glows long after the flames have gone.

@image commons:File:Jupiter's Magnetosphere animation.png
That deep ocean of metallic hydrogen moves and swirls, and as it moves, it creates a magnetic field. Jupiter's magnetic field is the strongest of any planet. It is many times stronger than the Earth's.

Around Jupiter, this field makes an invisible bubble in space, called a magnetosphere. It is enormous. If we could see it from Earth, it would look larger in our sky than the full Moon. And on the side away from the Sun, it stretches out in a long tail that reaches as far as the orbit of Saturn.

@image commons:File:Hubble Captures Vivid Auroras in Jupiter's Atmosphere.jpg
Like the Earth, Jupiter has northern and southern lights, called auroras. But they are much larger and much brighter. Glowing rings of light circle the poles, mostly in colors that our eyes cannot see, such as ultraviolet.

On Earth, auroras are mostly painted by the wind of particles from the Sun. On Jupiter, the Sun plays a part, too. But a great deal of the material comes from somewhere much closer. It comes from one of Jupiter's own moons, Io, which we will meet a little later.

@image commons:File:JWST Jupiter - Flickr - geckzilla.png
And here, in the middle of our journey, a small and gentle note. If these calm stories help you rest, you are always welcome to subscribe to Relaxing Pills, so you can find the next one easily. And now, let us go back to the quiet glow of Jupiter.

Close to Jupiter, its magnetic field also traps fast-moving particles, in belts that circle the planet. This radiation is very strong, and it is hard on machines.

@image commons:File:Jupiter 's Circumpolar Cyclones - North Pole - Nasa's Juno PJ47 (52621337340).png
Spacecraft that visit Jupiter must carry thick shields to protect their electronics. Even so, over many years, the radiation wears them down. Juno's camera, for example, became weaker and less sharp near the end of its long journey.

But Juno did send back one more beautiful surprise from the poles. Around the north pole of Jupiter, there are eight large storms, arranged in a ring, like petals around the center of a flower.

@image commons:File:PIA21641-Jupiter-SouthernStorms-JunoCam-cropped.jpg
Around the south pole, there is a similar pattern, at first with five storms circling one in the middle. In 2019, a new, smaller storm squeezed into the ring and joined the dance.

Nobody expected this. On Saturn, there is a single great storm at each pole. On Jupiter, there are crowns of storms, turning slowly together, holding their pattern year after year, in the cold, dim light at the top and bottom of the world.

# The Gentle Giant That Shaped the Solar System
@image commons:File:Protoplanetary Disk (31409065484).png
Let us travel back in time now, about four and a half billion years. There is no Earth yet. There is no Jupiter. There is only a young Sun, and around it, a wide, flat disk of gas and dust, slowly turning, like a great soft cloud stirred in a cup.

In this disk, tiny grains of dust touch and stick together. Grains become pebbles. Pebbles become rocks. Slowly, over many thousands of years, the first small worlds begin to form.

@image commons:File:Artist's impression of a gas giant planet forming in the disc around the young star HD 100546 (eso1310a).tiff
Far from the young Sun, where it was cold enough for ice to form, there was much more solid material to build with. There, a core grew quickly. And when it became heavy enough, it began to pull in the gas around it, hydrogen and helium, in enormous amounts.

This was the beginning of Jupiter. Many scientists believe it was the very first planet to form in our Solar System.

@image commons:File:Iron meteorite. Hammersley Range, Australia-8911.jpg
How can anyone know this? Part of the answer comes from a surprising place: meteorites. These are small pieces of rock and iron that fall to Earth from space. Some of them are older than any rock on our own planet.

In 2017, a team of scientists studied tiny differences in the metals inside iron meteorites. They found that the meteorites came from two separate families, which formed in two different parts of the early disk, and stayed apart for millions of years.

@image commons:File:HL Tau protoplanetary disk.jpg
Something must have kept them apart. The best explanation, the scientists said, was Jupiter. Its growing gravity opened a wide gap in the disk, like a path cleared through tall grass. Material on one side could not easily cross to the other.

If this is right, Jupiter's core had already grown to about twenty times the mass of the Earth within the first million years. That is very early, in a story that has lasted four and a half billion years.

@image commons:File:Pioneer 10 Crosses the Asteroid Belt.jpg
Some scientists also think that Jupiter did not always stay in the same place. In some ideas, the young planet moved inward toward the Sun, and then back out again, before settling where it is today, around four billion years ago.

If it wandered like this, it would have stirred the small rocky and icy bodies around it, sending some inward and some outward. This is still debated. But many scientists agree that Jupiter's gravity helped to shape the asteroid belt, the wide ring of rocks between Mars and Jupiter.

@image commons:File:High-resolution global view of Vesta in natural color processed from archival data.jpg
Inside that belt, Jupiter's pull is like a gentle, steady push, repeated again and again. Because of it, the belt never gathered together into one more planet. And in some places, where an asteroid would circle the Sun in step with Jupiter, the rocks were gently pushed aside, leaving quiet empty lanes.

Some scientists even think that, long ago, Jupiter helped send icy bodies toward the inner Solar System, and that a few of them may have brought water to the young Earth. This, too, is still being studied.

@image commons:File:Illustration of Asteroid Belts (2021-005).tif
Jupiter also has travelling companions. Along its path around the Sun, there are two great swarms of asteroids. One swarm goes about sixty degrees ahead of Jupiter. The other follows about sixty degrees behind. They are called the Trojan asteroids, and most of them are named after heroes from the old Greek stories of the Trojan War.

They are held there in a gentle balance between the pull of the Sun and the pull of Jupiter, like boats resting in calm water behind a harbor wall.

@image commons:File:NHQ202110160002 - Lucy Spacecraft Launch.jpg
These asteroids may be very old pieces, left over from the time when the planets were forming. That is why a NASA spacecraft named Lucy is now on its way to visit them. It was launched in October 2021, and it should reach the first Trojan asteroids in 2027.

The mission is named after a famous ancient fossil skeleton, called Lucy, that helped scientists understand the human story. In the same way, these old rocks may help us understand the story of the planets.

@image commons:File:Photo Illustration of Comet P-Shoemaker-Levy 9 and Planet Jupiter (1994-26-167).tiff
Jupiter's gravity also changes the paths of comets. In July nineteen ninety-four, astronomers all over the world watched as the pieces of a comet, called Shoemaker-Levy 9, fell into Jupiter's clouds, one after another, over several days. They left dark marks in the clouds, which faded over the following months.

For scientists, it was a rare chance to learn what Jupiter's atmosphere is made of, and to watch, for the first time, two bodies of our Solar System meeting.

@image commons:File:Comet-Hale-Bopp-29-03-1997 hires adj.jpg
Some people like to say that Jupiter protects the Earth, catching comets like a great goalkeeper. The truth is more complicated. Jupiter catches some comets, but its gravity can also send others toward the inner planets. Scientists still discuss which effect is larger.

So perhaps the kindest way to say it is this. Jupiter is the great gardener of the Solar System. For billions of years, it has quietly arranged things, moving a little here and a little there, shaping the place where we live.

# The Moons of Jupiter: Io, Europa, Ganymede and Callisto
@image commons:File:Galilean satellites noborder.jpg
Jupiter is not alone in its part of space. It is surrounded by a whole family of moons. Today, astronomers recognize more than a hundred of them, and the number keeps growing, as telescopes find more small ones far from the planet.

Most of these moons are tiny, only a few kilometers wide, perhaps rocks that wandered too close long ago and were caught. But four of them are large, real worlds of their own. They are the four moons that Galileo saw. Io, Europa, Ganymede and Callisto.

@image commons:File:Io highest resolution true color.jpg
Let us begin with Io, the closest of the four. Io is a little larger than our own Moon. But it does not look like our Moon at all. It is yellow and orange and white, with dark spots and red rings, like a pizza, some people say, or like a painter's palette left out in the sun.

Io is the most volcanically active world in the whole Solar System. Hundreds of volcanoes cover its surface.

@image commons:File:PIA01081-Color Mosaic and Active Volcanic Plumes on Io.jpg
Some volcanoes on Io send soft, blue-white plumes of gas and dust high above the surface, rising into space in tall, umbrella shapes, and then falling slowly back down. Others hold glowing lakes of lava. The surface is always being painted again, covered with fresh layers of sulfur and rock.

Voyager 1 discovered these volcanoes in 1979. It was the first time anyone had seen an active volcano on a world other than the Earth.

@image commons:File:Jupiter, perijove 22 Io shadow composite.jpg
Why is Io so warm inside, when it is so far from the Sun? The answer is a quiet tug of war. Io, Europa and Ganymede move around Jupiter in a kind of rhythm. Each time Ganymede goes around once, Europa goes around twice, and Io four times.

Because of this rhythm, Io's path is pulled very slightly out of a perfect circle. Jupiter's enormous gravity stretches Io a little, then relaxes, then stretches it again, like hands gently squeezing a ball of clay. All this squeezing makes heat. And the heat comes out as volcanoes.

@image commons:File:PIA19048 realistic color Europa mosaic edited.jpg
Next comes Europa. Europa is a little smaller than our Moon, and it looks completely different from Io. Its surface is bright and smooth, made of ice, and crossed by long brown and red lines, like cracks in the frozen surface of a lake.

There are very few craters on Europa. That tells scientists that its surface is young. Something has been renewing it, smoothing over the old marks.

@image commons:File:Europa Ice Rafts.jpg
Beneath the ice, scientists believe, there is a deep ocean of salty liquid water. The Galileo spacecraft found strong signs of it, in the way Europa reacted to Jupiter's magnetic field. And this ocean may hold more water than all the oceans of the Earth put together.

Water, warmth from the squeezing of Jupiter, and perhaps some of the chemicals that life needs. That is why Europa is one of the most interesting places in the Solar System to look for life beyond the Earth.

@image commons:File:Ganymede - Perijove 34 Composite.jpg
Next is Ganymede, the largest moon in the entire Solar System. It is even larger than the planet Mercury. If it travelled around the Sun on its own, we might call it a planet.

Its surface is a patchwork of dark, old regions and lighter regions covered with long grooves and ridges. It is like an old map, folded and unfolded many times.

@image commons:File:Ganymede terrain.jpg
Ganymede is special in another way. It is the only moon we know that makes its own magnetic field. And deep beneath its ice, it may also hide an ocean of salty water, perhaps sandwiched between layers of ice.

So inside one quiet, frozen moon, there may be a hidden sea, dark and still, which no one has ever seen.

@image commons:File:Cratered plains PIA00745.jpg
Finally, there is Callisto, the farthest of the four large moons. Callisto is almost as large as Mercury. Its surface is dark and old, and covered with craters, one beside another, almost everywhere you look.

Callisto has hardly changed for a very long time. It is not squeezed by Jupiter as strongly as the others, so it has no volcanoes and no fresh ice flowing over its face. It is a quiet, sleeping world.

@image commons:File:Callisto mosaic by Voyager 1 PIA00080.jpg
One of Callisto's oldest marks is a huge basin called Valhalla, with rings around it, like the ripples that spread when you drop a stone into a still pond. But here, the ripples froze long ago, and never moved again.

Even Callisto, scientists think, may hide some liquid water deep beneath its surface. If so, Jupiter may have three ocean moons. Three hidden seas, each one far from the Sun, each one dark and quiet.

@image commons:File:Galileo Amalthea artwork.jpg
Closer to Jupiter than all four, there are a few small inner moons. One of them, Amalthea, is red and long, shaped a little like a potato. It is only about two hundred and fifty kilometers long. Other small moons are farther out, on wide, tilted paths, sometimes going around Jupiter in the opposite direction.

And around the planet, very faint, there are rings.

@image commons:File:The Rings of Jupiter (39392026834).jpg
Jupiter's rings are nothing like Saturn's bright, wide rings. They are thin and dark, made mostly of very fine dust. Voyager 1 discovered them in 1979. Later, the Galileo spacecraft showed that the dust comes from small inner moons, knocked off their surfaces by tiny meteorites.

They are hard to see, except when the Sun shines from behind them. Then they glow softly, like dust floating in a sunbeam in a quiet room.

# Visitors from Earth: Pioneer, Voyager, Galileo and Juno
@image commons:File:An artist's impression of a Pioneer spacecraft on its way to interstellar space.jpg
For almost all of human history, Jupiter was something we could only watch from far away. Then, in the twentieth century, we began to send machines to visit it.

The first was Pioneer 10. It was launched in March nineteen seventy-two, and it travelled for about twenty-one months. Many people had worried that it might not survive the crossing of the asteroid belt. But it passed through safely. On the fourth of December, nineteen seventy-three, it flew past Jupiter.

@image commons:File:Photograph by Pioneer 10 Jupiter at a range of 2584000km (ref- image number sequence A-51 (before) during the 4 days on either side of closest approach) moon seen on upper right on image (ARC-1973-AC73-9253).jpg
Pioneer 10 sent back the first close pictures of the giant. They were simple images, but nobody had ever seen Jupiter from so near. A year later, its twin, Pioneer 11, flew even closer, and then went on toward Saturn.

Both spacecraft then kept travelling outward, away from the Sun. Pioneer 10's last faint signal reached the Earth in 2003, from more than twelve billion kilometers away.

@image commons:File:Voyager spacecraft (cropped).jpg
In nineteen seventy-nine came the two Voyager spacecraft. Voyager 1 flew past Jupiter in March, and Voyager 2 followed in the summer. Their cameras were much better, and the pictures they sent back changed everything.

For the first time, people could see the clouds of Jupiter moving, swirling and flowing around the Great Red Spot. Scientists put the images together into short films, and watched the weather of another world.

@image commons:File:Jupiter - Voyager 1 (46027301945).png
The Voyagers found the thin rings of Jupiter. They found new small moons. And they found the volcanoes of Io, which surprised everyone.

Then both spacecraft travelled on, toward Saturn, and much later, out into the space between the stars. They are still out there today, very far away, still sending us faint messages from the edge of the Sun's home.

@image commons:File:Galileo spacecraft & IUS deployment sequence in OV-104's payload bay on STS-34 (S34-71-000AK).jpg
Next came Galileo, named after the man who first saw Jupiter's moons. It was launched in October nineteen eighty-nine, and after a long and winding journey, it arrived at Jupiter in December nineteen ninety-five.

Galileo was not a quick visitor. It went into orbit and stayed. For almost eight years, it travelled around Jupiter, again and again, passing close to all four large moons.

@image commons:File:Artwork Galileo-Io-Jupiter.JPG
It sent its small probe into the clouds. It watched Io's volcanoes change. And it found the strongest signs yet of the hidden ocean beneath the ice of Europa.

At the end, in September two thousand and three, the scientists made a careful choice. They guided Galileo into Jupiter itself, where it would disappear safely. This was to protect Europa. They did not want the spacecraft, one day, to drift onto that icy moon and bring tiny passengers from Earth into a place that might have its own life.

@image commons:File:Portrait of Jupiter from Cassini.jpg
Other spacecraft visited on their way to somewhere else. In late two thousand, the Cassini spacecraft passed by on its way to Saturn. Over a few months, it took around twenty-six thousand pictures of Jupiter, making the most detailed portrait of the planet that anyone had seen until then.

In two thousand and seven, the New Horizons spacecraft also passed by, on its long road to Pluto, and took new pictures of Io's volcanoes and the faint rings.

@image commons:File:One of Juno's solar panels.jpg
Then came Juno. Juno is named after the Roman goddess who was the wife of Jupiter. In the old story, Jupiter hid behind a veil of clouds, and Juno could see through them. In the same way, the spacecraft was built to see beneath Jupiter's clouds.

Juno arrived on the fourth of July, two thousand and sixteen. It flies on long, stretched orbits, which carry it far away from Jupiter and then swing it very close, over the poles, skimming above the clouds.

@image commons:File:Jupiter - Northern Hemisphere - Nasa’s Juno PJ 34 (51318315034).png
Juno carried a small camera, called JunoCam. And the scientists did something lovely with it. They shared the raw pictures with the public. People all over the world, at home, on their own computers, could download them and turn them into beautiful images.

Many of the most famous pictures of Jupiter's swirling blue and white clouds were made in this way, by citizen scientists, working with patience and care.

@image commons:File:Jupiter Southern Hemisphere - Juno (34724022251).png
For more than nine years, Juno studied Jupiter. It measured its gravity and its magnetic field. It found the fuzzy core, the deep roots of the Great Red Spot, and the crowns of storms at the poles. It also flew close past Ganymede, Europa and Io, and sent back new pictures of them.

Little by little, the radiation wore it down. But what it taught us will stay.

@image commons:File:Europa Clipper spacecraft best 140912jpg.jpg
And now, as you listen, two new visitors are already on their way. The first is NASA's Europa Clipper. It was launched in October 2024, and it is the largest spacecraft NASA has ever built to explore another planet. With its solar panels open, it is about thirty meters wide.

It should arrive at Jupiter in 2030. Then, over several years, it will fly past Europa dozens of times, looking at its ice, and listening for signs of the ocean below.

@image commons:File:Juice flyby of Callisto artists impression.jpg
The second visitor comes from Europe. It is called Juice, the Jupiter Icy Moons Explorer, from the European Space Agency. It was launched in April 2023, and it should reach Jupiter in 2031.

After flying past Ganymede, Callisto and Europa many times, Juice will slow down and go into orbit around Ganymede itself. It will be the first spacecraft ever to orbit a moon in the outer Solar System. Two new travellers, moving quietly through the dark, toward the gentle giant.

# Finding Jupiter in Your Own Night Sky
@image commons:File:Van der Neer - Moonlit Landscape with Bridge.jpg
After all these journeys, here is something gentle and simple. You do not need a spacecraft to visit Jupiter. On many clear nights of the year, you can see it yourself, with nothing but your own eyes.

Look for a bright, steady light, brighter than almost any star, with a soft cream color. It does not flicker much. It shines calmly. If you see such a light, and it is not the Moon or Venus, there is a good chance that it is Jupiter.

@image commons:File:Texas and Instruments.jpg
Every thirteen months or so, the Earth passes between the Sun and Jupiter. At those times, Jupiter rises in the east as the Sun sets in the west, and it stays in the sky all night long, bright and high.

If you have a pair of ordinary binoculars, try holding them very still, perhaps resting your arms on a wall or a fence. Around the bright planet, you may see one, two, three or four tiny points of light, sitting in a line.

@image commons:File:Galileo manuscript.png
Those little points are Io, Europa, Ganymede and Callisto. You are seeing them in the same way that Galileo saw them, more than four hundred years ago. And if you look again the next night, you will see that they have moved.

There is something very peaceful in that. The same four moons, still turning, still keeping their old rhythm. Galileo watched them. Rømer timed them. And tonight, perhaps, you can watch them too.

@image commons:File:Voyager 1 Image of Jupiter with Io and Europa.jpg
Remember, too, that the light you see is old. The sunlight that falls on Jupiter's clouds then travels across space to reach your eyes. Depending on where the planets are, that journey takes between about thirty-five and fifty-two minutes.

So when you look at Jupiter, you never see it exactly as it is. You see it as it was, a little while ago. A calm, golden picture, sent across the dark, just for whoever happens to look up.

# Goodnight, Jupiter
@image commons:File:Jupiter - PJ30 - Composite (50621963507).png
And now, let us slow down, and let the journey come to an end.

Somewhere far away, beyond Mars, beyond the asteroid belt, Jupiter is turning. Its long belts of cloud are drifting east and west. Its old red storm is turning slowly in the south. There are no sounds that we could hear, only the quiet movement of air, and light, and time.

@image commons:File:Jupiter's Moons - Family Portrait 050107 09.jpg
Around it, the moons are moving too. Io, warm and bright. Europa, white and smooth, with its hidden ocean resting beneath the ice. Ganymede, large and patient. Callisto, dark and old, sleeping in its craters.

Each one keeps its place. Each one goes around and around, as it has done for billions of years, and as it will do for billions of years more. Nothing is in a hurry out there.

@image commons:File:Pale Blue Dot from Voyager 1 - PIA23645.tiff
And far away, much closer to the Sun, there is a small blue world, the Earth. On that world, tonight, there is a room. And in that room, there is you, resting, warm and safe.

Let your breathing be slow and easy. Let your body grow heavy. There is nothing more to learn tonight, nothing more to remember. The giant will still be there tomorrow.

@image commons:File:The Kingdom of Orion (ann22042a).jpg
Let Jupiter be a soft light in your mind now. A gentle, golden point, turning slowly in the dark. Its clouds move like slow rivers. Its moons drift around it, like small boats on a quiet sea.

Rest now. Let the stars keep watch. And let the gentle giant go on turning, far away, while you drift into sleep.

Goodnight.
