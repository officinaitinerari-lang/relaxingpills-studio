# Welcome to a Quiet Day in Ancient Rome
@image commons:File:Claude Lorrain, The Roman Forum (Le Campo Vaccino), 1636, NGA 47662.jpg
Hello, and welcome to Relaxing Pills. Find a comfortable position. Let your shoulders fall a little lower, and let your breath slow down. There is nothing you need to do tonight. You only need to listen.

Tonight we travel to ancient Rome. This is ancient Rome for sleep: a slow walk through one ordinary day in the greatest city of the ancient world, around the second century, in the age of the emperors. We will not follow kings or battles. We will follow bakers and schoolchildren, water and bread, warm baths and quiet lamps. We will wake with the city before the sun, and we will lie down with it again when the stars come out.

So close your eyes, if you like. The streets are still dark. And somewhere in the distance, a cart is rolling slowly over the stones.

# Before Dawn: The City That Never Quite Sleeps
@image commons:File:Insula reconstrustion. Casa di Diana. Gismondi.JPG
It is the last hour of the night. Above the seven hills, the sky is still deep blue, almost black. A few stars hang over the rooftops. The air is cool, and it smells of wood smoke, wet stone and the river.

Rome is enormous. Historians still debate the exact number, but many believe that perhaps around a million people lived here at its height. No city in Europe would be this large again for well over a thousand years. And yet, at this hour, most of those people are asleep. They lie on simple beds, on straw mattresses, on mats on the floor, wrapped in wool blankets against the cool air.

But the city is not silent. Listen. There is a low rumble, coming and going. It is the sound of wheels.

@image search:Roman cart relief sculpture
In Rome, heavy carts were not allowed to move through the crowded streets during most of the day. A law from the time of Julius Caesar, which survives today on a bronze tablet found in southern Italy, says that wagons may not be driven in the city after sunrise, until about the tenth hour of the day. There were a few exceptions. Carts could carry stone for temples and public buildings. Carts could carry away rubble. And on special days, carts could take part in religious processions and public games. But for ordinary goods, the rule was simple. Deliveries came at night.

So in the dark hours, the city works. Carts bring sacks of grain, jars of oil and wine, bundles of firewood, blocks of stone and loads of vegetables from the farms outside the walls. Ox carts and donkey carts creak and sway. Drivers call softly to their animals. Iron rims grind against paving stones.

@image search:Juvenal satires engraving
Not everyone loved this. The poet Juvenal, who lived in Rome around this time, complained that the noise of the carts at night could wake a sleeping seal, or even an emperor. Romans, it seems, worried about traffic and noise, just as people in big cities do today. There is something comforting in that. Two thousand years ago, someone else lay awake in the dark, listening to the street, and wishing it would be quiet.

But tonight, the sound is gentle. It rises and falls like waves on a distant shore. And slowly, the carts begin to leave. The tenth hour of night has passed. Dawn is coming.

@image commons:File:La maison de Diane (Ostia Antica) (5901008906).jpg
Most Romans did not live in grand houses. They lived in tall apartment blocks called insulae. The word means "islands," perhaps because each block stood alone, surrounded by streets on every side, like an island in a sea of stone.

A list of the city's buildings from the early fourth century counts more than forty-four thousand insulae, and fewer than two thousand private houses. Scholars still discuss exactly what the word insula meant in that list. It may have meant whole buildings, or perhaps single apartments. But either way, the message is clear. Most of Rome lived in apartments.

These buildings were often four or five floors high, sometimes more. Under the emperor Augustus, the government tried to limit their height, because tall buildings made of brick and wood were not always safe. On the ground floor, facing the street, there were shops. Behind the shops, and up a set of narrow stairs, there were the homes.

@image commons:File:Ostia antica - Casa di Diana 01.JPG
Here is something that surprises many people today. In a Roman insula, the best apartments were on the lower floors. The ground floor and the first floor had larger rooms, higher ceilings, and sometimes even painted walls and mosaic floors. Water was close. The stairs were few. As you climbed higher, the rooms became smaller and cheaper. At the very top, under the roof tiles, lived the poorest tenants, in tiny rooms that were hot in summer and cold in winter.

Today, in many cities, the top floor with the view is the most expensive. In ancient Rome, it was the opposite. The view was not worth the stairs.

We can still walk inside buildings like these. Not in Rome itself, where most were lost or built over, but in Ostia, the old port of Rome, near the sea. There, the brick walls of apartment blocks still stand two or three floors high. You can climb ancient stairs, look through ancient windows, and touch the same bricks that Roman tenants touched every day.

# First Light: Waking Up in a Roman Home
@image commons:File:Giovanni Paolo Panini - A Capriccio of the Roman Forum - 1964.41 - Yale University Art Gallery.jpg
The sky turns from black to grey, and from grey to a soft, pale gold. The first birds begin to sing from the rooftops and the gardens. Somewhere a dog barks once and then goes quiet.

For the Romans, the day began with the sun. They divided the daylight into twelve hours, from sunrise to sunset. The first hour began at dawn. The sixth hour ended at noon. The twelfth hour ended at sunset. The night was divided into twelve parts as well.

This means that a Roman hour was not always the same length. In summer, when the days were long, each hour of daylight was long too, perhaps around seventy-five minutes. In winter, when the days were short, each hour shrank to around forty-five minutes. Romans did not mind. Their lives followed the light, not the clock.

@image search:Roman sundial ancient stone
To tell the time, they used sundials. One of the first public sundials in Rome was brought from a city in Sicily, and it was not quite right for Rome's position on the earth. For many years, the Romans used it anyway. Later, they built sundials made for their own city. On cloudy days, and at night, they could use water clocks, which measured time by the slow, steady flow of water from one container into another.

But most people did not need a clock to wake up. They woke with the light, with the birds, with the noise of the street, and with the smell of bread from the bakery below.

@image search:Roman tunic wall painting Pompeii
Getting ready in the morning was simple. Many Romans slept in a light tunic, or in their underclothes. When morning came, they simply stood up, splashed a little water on their face and hands, and put on a fresh tunic for the day. The tunic was a simple garment, like a long shirt, made from wool or linen, tied at the waist with a belt. Men usually wore it to the knee. Women wore longer tunics, and often a long dress over them, called a stola if they were married citizens.

There was no long morning shower. The proper bath would come later, in the afternoon. That was the Roman way.

@image search:Roman toga statue marble
If a man was a citizen and had business in the city, he might also put on a toga. The toga was a huge piece of white wool, many meters long, wrapped carefully around the body and over one shoulder. It was elegant. It was also heavy, warm, and difficult to put on alone. Many Romans complained about it. But it was the dress of a citizen, and on formal occasions, it was expected.

@image commons:File:Pompeii - House of the Vetti - 4.jpg
Now let us leave the busy insula for a moment and step into a different kind of home. This is a domus, the private house of a wealthier family. From the street, it does not look like much. The outside walls are plain, with only a few small windows. A Roman house turned its face inward, away from the noise.

But step through the front door, and the world changes. You walk through a short, narrow passage, and then you are in the atrium. It is a tall, quiet hall, open to the sky in the middle. Through that opening in the roof, rain falls into a shallow pool in the floor, called the impluvium. In the early morning, the pool reflects the pale sky like a mirror.

@image search:Pompeii atrium impluvium house
Around the atrium are small rooms. Some are bedrooms, called cubicula. Some are storerooms. Straight ahead, opening onto the atrium, is a larger room called the tablinum. This is where the master of the house might sit to receive visitors. Beyond it, if the family is rich, there may be a garden surrounded by columns, called a peristyle. Fountains splash softly there. Flowers grow in neat beds. The walls are painted in deep reds, soft yellows and gentle blues, with images of gardens, birds, landscapes and old myths.

The house is waking. Enslaved servants sweep the floors and open the shutters. In the kitchen, someone blows gently on the coals of the hearth until a small flame comes back to life.

@image commons:File:Casa dei Vettii - Larario.jpg
Before the day truly begins, many families pause at a small shrine. It is called the lararium. In a rich house, it may look like a tiny temple, with columns and a little roof. In a modest home, it may be only a painting on the kitchen wall.

In the painting, two young figures dance on their toes, holding a drinking horn and a shallow bowl. They are the Lares, the gods who protect the house and the family. Often, below them, a painted snake moves calmly through green plants. To the Romans, the snake was not frightening. It was a sign of the spirit of the place, a guardian of the home.

A member of the family might leave a small offering here in the morning: a few grains, a little wine, a pinch of incense, or a flower. A quiet word. A wish for a good day. Then the house turns to the world outside.

# The Morning Visit: Patrons and Clients
@image commons:File:Pompeii - Via dell'Abbondanza.jpg
Outside the door of the domus, even at this early hour, people are already waiting. Some stand quietly. Some talk in low voices. Most of them are wearing togas. They have come to say good morning.

This was one of the most Roman of all Roman customs. It was called the salutatio, the morning greeting. In Roman society, many people were bound together in a relationship between a patron and his clients. The patron was a man of wealth or influence. The clients were people who depended on him in some way. They might be freedmen, formerly enslaved people who had been set free. They might be poorer citizens, small traders, young men starting their careers, or people from the countryside who needed help in the city.

@image search:Roman atrium house Pompeii painting
Each morning, the clients came to the patron's house, soon after dawn. They waited in the atrium, among the shadows and the painted walls, until the patron was ready to see them. Then, one by one, or in small groups, they stepped forward to greet him.

Some came simply to show respect. Some came with a request. Perhaps they needed advice about a legal problem. Perhaps they needed a loan, a letter of recommendation, or help finding work. The patron listened. He might help, or he might promise to think about it.

In return, the clients gave the patron something just as valuable to him: their support. They voted for him if he stood for office. They spoke well of him in the city. And when he left the house, they walked with him.

@image search:Roman relief procession togas
This walk was important. After the morning greetings, the patron often went down to the Forum, the great public square at the heart of the city, to do his business. His clients walked around him, a crowd of white togas moving through the narrow streets. The bigger the crowd, the more important the man looked. People in the street would notice. A long line of clients was like a sign that said: this man matters.

Sometimes, the patron gave his clients a small gift, called the sportula. The word means "little basket." In earlier times, it may really have been a basket of food. Later, it was often a small amount of money. For a poor client, this daily gift could make a real difference.

@image search:Seneca bust marble
Not every Roman thought this custom was beautiful. The philosopher Seneca, who lived in the first century, wrote that many people visited the rich not out of friendship, but in the hope of money or favors. And some clients complained about standing in the cold early morning, in their heavy togas, waiting for a door to open. A busy client might hurry from one patron's house to another, collecting greetings like a postman collecting letters.

But for many people, the morning visit was simply part of life. It was how Rome worked. It wove the great city together with thousands of small threads, from rich to poor, from house to house, every single morning.

@image search:Roman atrium painting nineteenth century
Imagine standing among them. The atrium is cool and dim. The pool in the floor holds a small square of morning sky. A servant moves quietly between the visitors. You can hear the soft rustle of wool, the low murmur of voices, the distant calls of the street outside. Someone coughs. Someone laughs softly. The door to the tablinum opens, and a name is called.

It is your turn. You step forward, say good morning, and wish the patron well. He nods, says a few kind words, and turns to the next visitor. And just like that, your duty for the morning is done.

Now you are free to go out into the city. And you are probably a little hungry.

# Bread and Breakfast in Ancient Rome
@image commons:File:Sale bread MAN Napoli Inv9071 n01.jpg
The smell reaches you before you see the shop. Warm bread. It drifts out of an open doorway into the cool morning air, and it pulls people in from the street like a gentle hand.

The Roman breakfast was called the ientaculum. For most people, it was small and simple. A piece of bread, perhaps dipped in a little wine, or in olive oil, or in honey. Some cheese. A few olives. Perhaps some fruit, or something left over from last night's dinner. Many Romans ate it quickly, standing up, or on the way to work. Breakfast was not a grand event. It was a small kindness to the body before a long day.

@image commons:File:Tomb of Eurysaces the Baker - panorama.jpg
Bread was at the center of everything. Most people living in apartments did not have an oven of their own. A small brazier, perhaps, for warming food, but not a large oven for baking. So bread was usually made by professional bakers, in bakeries all over the city.

One of these bakers is still remembered today, because of his tomb. His name was Marcus Vergilius Eurysaces. He lived near the end of the Roman Republic, and he was very likely a freedman, someone who had once been enslaved and later became free, and successful. When he died, he built himself a large and unusual tomb, just outside one of the city gates. It still stands in Rome today.

@image commons:File:Tomb of Eurysaces the Baker - details.jpg
The tomb is covered with round openings that many people think look like the mouths of bread ovens or the vessels used to mix dough. And around the top runs a long carved band of stone, like a picture story. It shows the whole work of a bakery, step by step. Men bring in grain. Others weigh it. Donkeys turn the heavy millstones that grind the grain into flour. Workers sift the flour, knead the dough on long tables, shape the loaves, and slide them into the oven on long wooden paddles. Finally, the bread is weighed again and carried away.

It is a quiet, proud monument. It does not show a battle or a god. It shows work, and bread, and the people who made it.

@image search:Pompeii bakery millstones oven
In Pompeii, the town buried by the eruption of Mount Vesuvius in the year seventy-nine, archaeologists found many bakeries. Some still have their great stone millstones standing in the courtyard, shaped like giant hourglasses. Some still have their domed brick ovens.

And in the nearby town of Herculaneum, in excavations in nineteen thirty, archaeologists found something remarkable: a real loaf of Roman bread, still inside its oven. It had turned black and hard, like charcoal, but it kept its shape. It was round, and it was divided on top into segments, like slices of a cake, so it could be broken easily into portions. Roman loaves were often stamped with a mark before baking, perhaps to show which baker made them.

@image commons:File:Fresco portrait of the baker Terentius Neo and his wife, from Pompeii.jpg
From Pompeii, too, comes a famous painting of a man and a woman. They look out at us directly, calm and serious. He holds a scroll. She holds a writing tablet and a stylus pressed gently to her lips. The house where it was found is linked to a man named Terentius Neo, and because a bakery stood nearby, he is often called the baker. Historians still discuss exactly who the couple were. But whoever they were, they wanted to be remembered as educated people, as partners, side by side.

@image search:Ostia horrea warehouse ruins
Bread was so important in Rome that the state itself became involved. For a long time, the government arranged a monthly distribution of grain to a large number of male citizens living in the city. Under Augustus, around two hundred thousand people received this grain. It was not enough to feed a whole family in luxury, but it was a steady foundation, a promise that the city would not go hungry.

To keep this promise, Rome needed a constant flow of grain across the sea. Ships came from Egypt, from North Africa, from Sicily. They sailed to the harbors near the mouth of the Tiber River. There, the grain was unloaded, stored in great warehouses, and carried up the river on barges to the city.

@image search:Roman ship mosaic Ostia
So the bread in your hand this morning may have started its life in a field beside the Nile, far away in Egypt. It crossed the sea in a wooden ship. It rested in a warehouse at Ostia. It floated up the slow brown river. It was ground by a patient donkey, shaped by tired hands before dawn, and baked in a brick oven. And now, still warm, it is here, in the morning light.

You break it open. Steam rises. You take a slow bite.

# Water for a Million People
@image commons:File:Pont du Gard, a 40-60 AD? Roman aqueduct bridge that crosses the Gardon River, part of the Nîmes aqueduct, France (14701110126).jpg
After bread comes water. And in Rome, water came from far away.

Walk down almost any street in the city, and before long you will hear it: the soft, steady sound of running water. It falls from a stone spout into a stone basin, day and night, never stopping. These were the public fountains, and there were many of them, often on street corners. People came here to fill jars and buckets for their homes. Children came to drink. Animals were brought to drink as well. The water was cold and fresh, and it was free.

@image search:Aqua Claudia aqueduct painting
All of this water arrived through aqueducts. The first one was built in three hundred and twelve before our era. It was called the Aqua Appia, and it was mostly underground, a long hidden channel carrying water from springs outside the city. Over the next five centuries, the Romans built more and more. By the third century, eleven great aqueducts carried water into Rome.

When we think of aqueducts, we often imagine tall arches marching across the countryside. And some parts were like that. But most of the length of a Roman aqueduct ran underground, in channels cut through rock or built of stone and concrete. The arches were only used where the water had to cross a valley or a low plain.

@image search:Roman aqueduct Campagna painting nineteenth century
The secret of the aqueducts was a gentle slope. There were no pumps. The water simply flowed downhill, very slowly, over many kilometers. The engineers had to plan the slope with great care. Too steep, and the water would rush and damage the channel. Too flat, and the water would stop. Somehow, with simple tools, measuring rods and water levels, they made it work, across hills and valleys, for dozens of kilometers.

Near the end of the first century, a senator named Sextus Julius Frontinus was given the job of caring for Rome's water. He took his work very seriously. He wrote a book about the aqueducts, describing where each one came from, how much water it carried, and where it went. He also wrote, with some annoyance, about people who secretly tapped the pipes to steal water for their own gardens and houses. His book still survives. It is one of our best windows into this quiet, flowing world.

@image commons:File:Pont Du Gard Roman Aqueduct.jpg
Not all of Rome's engineering stayed in Rome. All across the empire, cities built their own aqueducts. One of the most beautiful still stands in the south of France. It is called the Pont du Gard. It carried water across a river on three levels of golden stone arches, toward the Roman town that is now called Nîmes. Today, people swim in the river beneath it on summer afternoons, and look up at the arches in the evening light.

The water that reached Rome was shared out carefully. First, it filled a settling tank, where sand and dirt could sink to the bottom. Then, it flowed into distribution tanks, and from there through lead and clay pipes to the fountains, to the public baths, and to the houses of those who had permission and could pay for a private supply.

@image search:Roman public latrine Ostia
Water also kept the city clean. It flowed through the public latrines, long stone benches where people sat side by side, chatting with neighbors, while water ran in a channel beneath them. It may seem strange to us now, but for the Romans it was ordinary and social. And all this used water flowed on, into drains and sewers, the greatest of which, the Cloaca Maxima, still carries water under the city today, after more than two thousand years.

Imagine resting your hand on the cool stone rim of a street fountain. The water runs over your fingers. It has traveled for days through the dark, under fields and hills, to reach this moment, here, in your hand. And it will keep on flowing, long after you have walked away.

# Children at School in Ancient Rome
@image commons:File:Funerary relief found in Neumagen near Trier, a teacher with three discipuli, around 180-185 AD, Rheinisches Landesmuseum Trier, Germany (29656302165).jpg
While the adults go to work, some of the children of Rome go to school. And Roman school began very early. Classes started at dawn and often went on until around noon.

There was no single school system in Rome, run by the state. Education was something families arranged and paid for themselves. So not every child went to school. Many poor children worked from a young age, helping their families in the shop, in the fields, or in the home. But many children, from many kinds of families, did learn to read and write, at least a little.

@image search:Roman school relief teacher students
For a child of around seven years old, the first teacher was called the litterator, or sometimes the ludi magister, the master of the school. The word for school was ludus, which is interesting, because the same word also meant play, or game.

These teachers were not rich, and they were not always respected. Often, they did not have a proper building. A teacher might rent a small room, or teach in the open space at the front of a shop, separated from the busy street by only a curtain. Some taught outside, at a street corner or under a covered walkway. Imagine learning your letters while carts and people and dogs and sellers pass by just a few steps away.

@image commons:File:Girl with stylus and tablets.Fresco found in Pompei.jpg
The children learned the alphabet first. Then syllables. Then whole words. They practiced writing on small wooden tablets covered with a thin layer of wax. With a pointed tool called a stylus, they scratched the letters into the soft wax. The other end of the stylus was flat, like a tiny spatula, so they could smooth the wax again and start over. It was a gentle, reusable notebook. They carried their tablets and tools in a small case.

They also learned to count and to do simple arithmetic, sometimes using small stones or counting on their fingers. The Latin word for a small stone is calculus. It is the root of our word "calculate."

@image search:Roman wax tablet stylus museum
Children often read aloud together, and recited lines from poems and old stories, over and over, until they knew them by heart. The classroom must have been a noisy place, full of young voices chanting in rhythm. One Roman poet, Martial, complained that the schoolmasters woke him before the roosters. We can imagine him in his upper floor apartment, pulling his blanket over his head.

Children from wealthier families were often taken to school by an enslaved attendant called a paedagogus. He carried the child's things, watched over them in the street, and sometimes helped them with their lessons at home. From that old word comes our modern word, pedagogy, the art of teaching.

@image commons:File:Fresco showing a woman so-called Sappho holding writing implements, from Pompeii, Naples National Archaeological Museum (14842101892).jpg
Girls also went to school, especially at this first level. Some learned to read and write very well. In paintings from Pompeii, we see young women holding writing tablets and styluses, thoughtful and calm, as if they are about to write a poem or a letter. But most girls stopped their studies early, because they were often married in their teens.

For boys whose families could pay, the next teacher was the grammaticus, from around the age of ten or eleven. He taught literature, both Latin and Greek. The students read the great poets, and learned to speak and write with care. And after that, a few young men studied with a rhetor, a teacher of public speaking, to prepare for life in the courts and in politics.

By the middle of the day, the morning lessons are over. The children close their tablets. They pack away their styluses. They step out into the bright street, blinking in the sun. And like children everywhere, in every century, they are very happy to be free.

# Shops, Streets and the Forum
@image commons:File:Pompeii Via dell' Abbondanza 01.jpg
By now, the city is fully awake. The streets are full of people. The narrow lanes between the insulae are shaded and cool, but the larger streets are bright and busy.

Along both sides of the street, there are shops. The Romans called them tabernae. Each one was a single room, open to the street through a wide doorway. At night, the doorway was closed with wooden shutters. In the morning, the shutters were taken down, and the shop opened like a flower.

@image search:Roman shop relief sculpture
Every kind of shop is here. A shoemaker sits on a low stool, working leather with a curved knife. A metalworker taps gently on a bronze pot. A barber shaves a customer, who sits very still. A cloth seller unrolls wool in rich colors, red, saffron, and deep green. A potter stacks his jars and lamps in neat rows. Behind many of these shops, or above them in a small loft, the shopkeeper and the family lived.

The streets themselves were paved with large, smooth blocks of hard stone, fitted together like a puzzle. On each side, there were raised sidewalks for people walking. In Pompeii, where the streets have survived, you can still see tall stepping stones that cross the road. They let people cross without stepping into the mud and the water that flowed along the street after rain. And you can still see grooves worn into the paving by the wheels of carts, year after year.

@image commons:File:Thermopolium in Pompeii.jpg
Around noon, many Romans ate a small lunch, called the prandium. Like breakfast, it was simple. And for many city people, it was eaten outside the home, at a kind of street food shop. Archaeologists call these shops thermopolia, a word that means "places that sell hot things." Romans themselves may have used other names.

A thermopolium had a counter facing the street, often covered with colorful painted plaster or pieces of marble. Set into the counter were large clay jars. In these jars, food was kept warm or cool: stews, lentils, beans, perhaps wine. A customer would stop, point, pay a few coins, and eat standing at the counter, or carry the food away.

@image commons:File:Pompeii, Ancient Roman fresco in the Thermopolium of Vetutius Placidus (48443395016).jpg
In Pompeii, one beautifully preserved food counter was uncovered only a few years ago, between twenty nineteen and twenty twenty. It was painted with bright images: ducks, a rooster, a dog, and a sea nymph riding a horse through the waves. Inside the jars, scientists found traces of the food that was sold there, including duck, pork, goat, fish and snails. It is like finding a little snack bar with the menu still written on the wall.

Now let us follow the crowd toward the center of the city, to the Forum.

@image commons:File:Giovanni Paolo Panini - An Architectural Capriccio of the Roman Forum with Philosophers and Soldiers among Ancient Ruins, in... - Google Art Project.jpg
The Roman Forum was the heart of Rome. It began, long ago, as a marshy valley between hills. Over many centuries, it became a great open space of stone, surrounded by temples, law courts and meeting halls.

Here, people did business, listened to speeches, attended trials, met friends and heard the news. Great buildings called basilicas stood along its sides. They were long, tall halls with rows of columns, where judges heard cases and merchants made deals, sheltered from the rain and the sun. Later, Christian churches would borrow the shape of these halls.

@image search:Roman Forum reconstruction painting
In the Forum, the sounds are many but soft from a distance. Voices rise from a law court where a speaker is making his case. Someone is reading a public notice aloud. Pigeons flutter between the columns. Footsteps echo on the stone.

Over the years, emperors built even larger forums nearby, each one grander than the last. The greatest was the Forum of Trajan, finished in the early second century. It had a huge open square, a vast basilica, two libraries, and a tall column covered in carved pictures, winding upward in a long spiral. That column still stands today, in the middle of modern Rome, with traffic flowing around it.

@image search:Trajan's Column engraving
Not far away, there were markets too. Some days were special market days, called nundinae. They came around every eighth day. On those days, farmers came into the city from the countryside to sell their produce, and people gathered in larger numbers to buy and sell and talk.

So the morning passes. You walk slowly through the crowd, looking at the shops, listening to the voices. The sun climbs higher. The shadows grow short. The middle of the day has come, and for many Romans, the working day is nearly over.

# An Afternoon at the Roman Baths
@image commons:File:Alma-Tadema A Favourite Custom 1909 Tate Britain.jpg
In the early afternoon, around the eighth or ninth hour, much of Rome put down its work and went to the baths. This was perhaps the most pleasant hour of the Roman day.

For the Romans, the baths were not only a place to get clean. They were a place to rest, to exercise, to meet friends, to talk, to read, to eat a snack, and to forget the noise of the streets for a while. There were small, private bathhouses all over the city, run as businesses. And there were the great public baths, the thermae, built by the emperors as gifts to the people. The entrance fee was usually tiny, a small coin, and sometimes there was no fee at all.

@image search:Baths of Caracalla ruins engraving
Let us walk into one of these great imperial baths. The building is enormous. Its walls rise high above us, covered with marble in many colors. There are gardens around it, with paths and trees and fountains. There are covered walkways where people stroll and talk in the shade.

The most famous of these buildings, the Baths of Caracalla, was finished in the early third century. It covered an area of many hectares. Up to around sixteen hundred people could bathe there at the same time, and perhaps several thousand in a single day. Even today, its ruined walls are so tall that, in summer, operas are performed among them under the open sky.

@image search:Roman bath apodyterium changing room ruins
The first room is the changing room, called the apodyterium. Along the walls, there are niches, like open lockers, where bathers leave their clothes. An enslaved attendant may watch over them, because thieves at the baths were a well known problem. The floor is cool marble under bare feet.

Many Romans began their visit with some exercise, in an open courtyard called the palaestra. Here, people stretched, ran, lifted weights, wrestled, or played ball games. There were several ball games, some played in a circle, passing the ball from hand to hand. Older men walked slowly around the edges, talking. Younger men ran and laughed. The sound of a ball slapping against a palm echoed off the walls.

@image commons:File:The Baths at Caracalla.jpg
Then the bathing began. There was no single fixed order, and people chose for themselves, but a typical visit moved through rooms of different warmth.

The tepidarium was the warm room. It was pleasant and gentle, neither hot nor cold, a place to sit and let the body slowly relax. Then came the caldarium, the hot room. Here, the air was heavy with steam, and pools of hot water filled the floor. The heat made the skin glow and the muscles soften.

How did the Romans heat these rooms? With a clever system called the hypocaust. The floors were raised up on small pillars of brick. Underneath, in the empty space, hot air from furnaces flowed and warmed the floor from below. Hot air also rose through hollow tiles inside the walls. In the Baths of Caracalla, around fifty furnaces burned under the building. Teams of enslaved workers fed them with wood all day long, in hot, dark tunnels far below the marble halls.

@image search:strigil ancient Roman bronze
The Romans did not use soap in the way we do. Instead, they rubbed olive oil into their skin. Then, with a curved metal tool called a strigil, they scraped the oil away, and with it the sweat and dust of the day. An attendant might do this for a richer bather. Others helped each other, or did it themselves. Some bathers enjoyed a massage with scented oil.

Finally, there was the frigidarium, the cold room. It was often the grandest hall of all, tall and bright, with cold plunge pools. After the heat of the caldarium, the cold water was a shock at first, and then a deep, cool calm. Some of the great baths also had a large outdoor swimming pool, called the natatio, where people swam slowly under the sky.

@image search:Roman bath frigidarium painting
And the baths offered more than water. In the Baths of Caracalla, there were two libraries, one for books in Greek, one for books in Latin, with shelves of scrolls set into niches in the walls. There were halls for lectures and conversation. There were sellers of snacks and drinks moving among the visitors. People played board games carved into the stone steps. Some came every single day, for hours.

The philosopher Seneca once lived in a room above a bathhouse. In a letter, he described all the noise that came up through his floor. He heard the grunts of men lifting weights, and the slap of the masseur's hands. He heard someone singing in the bath, and the calls of the snack sellers, each with his own special cry. It was not peaceful for him. But for the bathers below, it was simply the music of a good afternoon.

Imagine sitting on a warm marble bench in the tepidarium. The air is soft and slightly misty. Light falls from high windows in long golden beams. Somewhere, water is pouring gently into a basin. Voices echo softly under the high ceiling. Your body is warm and heavy and loose. There is nowhere else you need to be.

# The Evening Meal: Dinner in a Roman Home
@image commons:File:Roberto Bompiani - A Roman Feast - 72.PA.4 - J. Paul Getty Museum.jpg
As the sun begins to lean toward the west, Romans walk home from the baths, clean and relaxed. Now it is time for the most important meal of the day: the cena, or dinner.

In early times, the cena was eaten in the early afternoon. But over the centuries, it moved later and later, until it usually began in the late afternoon or early evening, after the baths. For many families, this was the one time of the day when everyone came together.

@image search:Roman triclinium Pompeii dining room
In a wealthy house, dinner was served in a special dining room called the triclinium. The name comes from the three couches arranged around a low table, in the shape of the letter U. The fourth side was left open, so that servants could bring and take away the dishes.

Wealthy Romans did not sit on chairs to eat. They lay down. Each couch usually held three diners, so a traditional dinner party had nine guests. They reclined on their left side, leaning on the left elbow, with soft cushions behind them. With their right hand, they took food from the table. Forks were not used for eating. Romans ate with their fingers, with spoons and with knives, and servants brought water and towels so that guests could wash their hands between courses.

@image search:Roman banquet fresco Pompeii
Not everyone could dine like this. In a small apartment at the top of an insula, a family might sit on stools around a simple table, or eat quickly with the bowl in their lap. Some poor families ate their evening meal at a cheap tavern or street counter. And for many people in the empire, dinner was a thick porridge, called puls, made from grain, cooked with water and perhaps a few vegetables, a little cheese or oil, and only rarely meat.

But tonight, let us imagine a comfortable dinner in a friendly home.

@image search:Roman still life fresco fruit
A proper Roman dinner came in three parts. First, there was the gustatio, the tasting course. This was light and fresh: eggs, olives, salads, mushrooms, and perhaps some shellfish. It was often served with mulsum, a sweet mixture of wine and honey.

Then came the main course. In a modest home, this might be a single dish of vegetables and beans with some fish or pork. At a rich dinner party, there might be several dishes, one after another. Roast meats, fish in sauces, birds, sausages, stuffed vegetables. Some rich Romans liked to surprise their guests with strange and showy dishes. But most dinners were much simpler, and the conversation mattered more than the food.

@image search:Roman mosaic fish
Finally came the second tables, the dessert. Fruit, nuts, honey cakes, dates, and sweet wine mixed with water. The Romans had a saying for something that lasted from beginning to end. They said it went "from the egg to the apples," because a dinner began with eggs and ended with fruit.

Many flavors were familiar. Olive oil. Honey. Herbs like mint, coriander and rue. But one flavor was very Roman indeed. It was a sauce made from fish, called garum. Small fish and fish parts were salted and left in the sun for many weeks, until they turned into a strong, salty liquid. It may sound strange to us, but it was used in a great many dishes, a little like salt or soy sauce is used today.

@image commons:File:The Garden Painting of the Villa of Livia at Prima Porta in Rome (30-20 BC), Palazzo Massimo alle Terme, Rome (8533507929).jpg
And many foods we think of as Italian today were not yet known in Rome. There were no tomatoes. No potatoes. No corn. No chili peppers. These would only arrive in Europe many centuries later, from the Americas. A Roman kitchen was filled instead with lentils and chickpeas, cabbages and leeks, onions and garlic, figs and grapes, pears and apples, and the deep green of olive trees.

Before the meal, or between the courses, the family often made a small offering to the household gods. A little food might be placed at the shrine, or dropped into the fire of the hearth. In this way, the gods of the home shared the family's table. It was a moment of thanks, small and quiet, repeated in thousands of homes across the city, every evening.

@image search:Roman dinner party painting nineteenth century
As the dinner goes on, the light outside turns golden, then pink, then grey. A servant lights the oil lamps. Guests talk about the day, about friends and family, about books, about the weather and the harvest. Someone reads a poem aloud. Someone plays a soft tune on a flute or a lyre. The conversation slows. The wine is mixed with plenty of water. The evening is long and calm.

# Twilight: A Quiet Evening at Home
@image commons:File:Lamp MET DP122.jpg
Darkness comes early in a Roman city, and it comes gently. There are no bright street lights. There are only small flames.

The Romans lit their homes with oil lamps. Most were small, made of clay, about the size of a hand. A lamp had a round body, filled with olive oil, and a short spout at the front, where a wick of linen or other fiber burned with a small, steady flame. On the top, there was often a little picture, pressed into the clay: a god, an animal, a ship, a flower, a scene from the theater. Rich families had lamps of bronze, sometimes hanging in groups from tall stands.

@image commons:File:Lamp MET DP808.jpg
The light was soft and golden, and it did not reach very far. Rooms filled with gentle shadows. Faces glowed warm in the circle of the flame. People did not stay up very late. Oil cost money, and the day would begin again at dawn.

So the evening was a time for small, quiet things. In the light of the lamp, a mother might spin wool, the thread turning slowly between her fingers. A father might check the household accounts on a wax tablet. Older children might practice their reading. Younger children played with simple toys: clay dolls, little wooden animals, spinning tops, and small hoops.

@image search:Roman knucklebones game ancient
One of the most popular games, for children and adults alike, was knucklebones. Players threw small bones, or pieces shaped like them, into the air, and tried to catch them on the back of the hand, or threw them like dice. Romans also loved board games. Some were games of strategy, a little like checkers. Others were played with dice, and the Romans enjoyed them very much, sometimes a little too much.

In a wealthy house, the family might take a last slow walk in the peristyle garden before bed. The fountain is quieter now. The air smells of damp earth and flowers. The painted walls are almost invisible in the dark, but a lamp in a doorway shows a glimpse of a painted bird on a painted branch.

@image commons:File:Villa of Livia - Garden fresco 001.jpg
One of the loveliest Roman paintings ever found shows exactly this kind of peace. It once covered all four walls of an underground room in a villa that belonged to Livia, the wife of the emperor Augustus. The painting shows a garden that never ends. There are fruit trees and flowering shrubs, laurel and pine, quince and pomegranate. Birds sit on the branches, or fly between them. A low fence of stone and lattice runs in front of the plants. Sitting in that cool room, you would have felt as if you were outside, in a garden on a summer day, at any time of year.

Today, this painting can be seen in a museum in Rome. People walk into the room and stop talking, and simply look.

@image commons:File:The Garden Painting of the Villa of Livia at Prima Porta in Rome (30-20 BC), detail with pine tree and pomegranate, Palazzo Massimo alle Terme, Rome (8534614956).jpg
Back in our imagined Roman home, the family gathers its things for the night. The children are washed and put to bed. Somewhere a door is closed and bolted. The fire on the hearth is banked down carefully, covered with ash so that it will keep a little heat until morning. The Romans considered the hearth the heart of the house, linked to the goddess Vesta, and many believed that its fire should not be allowed to go out.

The shutters of the shops in the street below are closed and locked. The last customers have gone home from the taverns. The city exhales.

# Night Falls Over Rome
@image commons:File:Giovanni Paolo Panini, Interior of the Pantheon, Rome, c. 1734, NGA 165.jpg
Now the sky is fully dark. Over the rooftops, the stars come out one by one, clear and bright, because there are so few lights on the ground to hide them.

Across the city, the great buildings stand silent. In the Forum, the temples are empty. In the Pantheon, the great temple with its round dome, a single circle of night sky looks down through the opening in the roof. The same opening is still there today, almost nineteen hundred years later. On rainy nights, the rain still falls straight through it, onto the marble floor below.

@image search:Roman vigiles firefighters relief
But even now, someone is awake. Through the dark streets walk the vigiles, the watchmen of Rome. The emperor Augustus created them as a kind of fire brigade and night watch. There were seven groups, called cohorts, each with many hundreds of men, perhaps around a thousand. Each cohort cared for two of the fourteen districts of the city.

They walked the streets at night carrying buckets, axes, ladders and blankets soaked with water. Their main job was to watch for fire and to stop it early, because in a city of wood and lamps, a small flame could spread. They also helped to keep order. Their quiet footsteps passing beneath the windows were a small comfort to those still awake. Someone was keeping watch over the city.

@image commons:File:Ostia, House of Diana (II) (4688458160).jpg
And far away, at the city gates, the carts are beginning to gather again. The rule of the day has ended. Ox carts and donkey carts roll slowly in from the dark countryside, carrying tomorrow's grain and wine and wood. The low rumble of wheels begins to move through the streets once more, rising and falling, like the sound of the sea.

High up in an insula, under the roof tiles, a tired worker turns over on a straw mattress and pulls a wool blanket over his shoulder. Below, in a comfortable apartment, a family sleeps close together. Across the city, in the domus, the atrium is dark and still. The small pool in the floor holds a few stars.

@image search:Roman countryside landscape painting evening
Imagine standing at a high window, looking out over Rome at night. Below you, the roofs spread out in every direction, dark shapes against the darker hills. Here and there, a tiny lamp still glows in a window. A dog barks once, far away, and then goes quiet. The river moves slowly through the city, silver under the moon.

Somewhere, water is running in a stone fountain. It ran this morning, when the bakers lit their ovens. It ran at noon, when the children came out of school. It ran in the afternoon, while the bathers rested in the warm rooms. And it runs now, in the dark, soft and steady, carrying the water of distant hills into the sleeping city.

@image search:Claude Lorrain landscape evening Roman
Two thousand years have passed since this night. The insulae have fallen. The togas are gone. The great baths are quiet ruins, where grass grows between the stones. And yet, so much of this day feels close to us. The smell of fresh bread in the morning. A child's sigh of relief when school is over. The pleasure of warm water after a long day. A good meal with people we love. A small lamp in the dark. The sound of the city, slowly falling asleep.

People in Rome lay down at night just as you are lying down now. They closed their eyes, and let the day go.
